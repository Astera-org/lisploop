
This past week I've wondered if the frame representation is correct and properly supportive of all the operations required, especially reorientation (conservation and symmetries to generative expressions, or vice-versa when learning laws and propagators), anti-unification (which acts upon expressions - but really it's a form of quotienting and trivial lifting under substitution), the very act of substitution itself (which is something like patterning of the dependency graph: an expression is a common pattern found in the execution graph, just lifted to independent contexts (the labeled residual, so to speak), with the remainder a constant or conserved expression).  This is what I was getting at in some of the later prompts - it's all lift, projection, fiber 'calculus', computation, substitution, conservation, and residuals.   Some of that made it to plan9.md.  All but the last are concretely topological = what connects to what, and can be learned in the process of executing the simulator, telling you explicitly through experimentation (holding all but a few constant) what is related to what through computation.  Algebra emerges from running parallel and depth >= 2 experiments on the simulator, showing that expressions themselves can be abstracted over in the same way, and the resulting regions of expression space are connected = equivalent under computation, for all sets of staged substitutions, conditional guards = calculated regions of base or lifted space where the law or propagator does not hold.   (A conditional is a partition: a disconnected region of space; here it's learned experimentally). 

All this topology - what is connected to what - I think can be re-expressed as a graph, hypergraph, or other datastructure, and can be computed too.  Making a tuple is an explicit example of instantiating a connection or edge, albeit one with coordinates.  Laws, algebras, functions, combinators, quotients, partitions (like guards) all are manipulations and abstractions of topological structure, observed or created from the input & output data.  Patterns occur independent of the 'level of abstraction' in the graph data, from the simply-connected numbers, their relation under arithmetic and elementary operations, to common patterns in functions and their applications (like recursion and looping, which require forms of lifting within the execution engine itself: **lift has to be a synthesizer primitive just like substitution is!**  Presently this is not true, it's wholly in the host lisp.)  
Reentrancy I think means freely shifting abstraction level when recognizing, transforming, simplifying, expanding a graph - whether that's an execution graph, a literal graph described by the data, graph generated by an AST, or some combination of all these (like label, lift, partition, quotient, reorder...).  
Reflection means that the graph of operations of the search becomes available as data to the synthesizer itself; as mentioned in the original prompts, this enables trivial problem avoidance, like doing the same calculation many times (there are a good number of guards for this in the kernel, afaik), to re-weighting of search strategies (pruning etc - policies that can be learned by reflection, instead of hard-coded). 

Yet (discrete) topology only goes so far; much data and computation are geometric, possessing a metric, and sometimes values areas, volumes, orientation, etc.  I feel that the original kernel suffered because it did not do enough to embrace both geometry and topology from the beginning, resulting in things like 'residuals' of frame becoming a bit clunky (though the algorithm used there might well be functional enough; I'm not sure if there are better options for bootstrapping generated and intermediate spaces other than pricing in the original or ambient spaces..).  It also makes things like policies & heuristics harder to naturally implement, as well as the not-yet-touched language component of the kernel.  In comparison, these things are quite natural in deep learning, where everything is geometrically embedded in some nearly-continuous high-dimensional space.  Gradient descent is adept at organizing geometric spaces, even if it is not necessarily sample-efficient at doing so.  We _must_ start with some bedrock of "these things are close" in addition to "these things are (topologically) connected".  The problem I see is that high-D embeddings are 'flat', whereas the native topology of graphs, hypergraphs, are very much folded and recursively nested.  This is very much a limitation anticipated but not overcome in the TBD language component of the proposals & intermediate work: in order for language to work, it needs to be compositional, where each word/atom modifies semantic meaning (which could be a 'like' term (proximity), an 'analogy' term (vector in some space), or 'action' (add a new space, do an operation between them).  The problem with deep learning is that all this is flat, forced into one uniformly-accessible geometric space (some high-D latent space); spectral elaboration enables some simplicity bias toward the representations, but it seems to me what is really needed is a hybrid geometry-topology engine.  This engine *makes* spaces where needed or instructed to (though the language, or from the underlying computational graph that it's trying to make a heuristic from, or when residuals require), collapses when some computational symmetry-finding lends evidence that the spaces are the same, and moves vectors around when suggested by ambient (time, space) or induced spaces (algorithmic similarity; proximity flows along verified topological links).  In the human brain, all might naively be considered to be geometrically one-level or 'flat': neurons have continuous firing rates, and you can't create neurons when you learn or invent a new concept (= heuristic handle on some partition of computational/residual space) – so there could be good reason for keeping the vectors flat high-D, and just doing what a transformer does, which is to change context to change the composition.  

Irregardless, I'd like to re-examine the fundamental datastructure of the kernel; all of above seems to be instances of adding, removing, splitting, combining linkages or dependencies, the continuous-space homologues of these, and reeentrant / reflective elaborations thereof (like patterns that control graph rewrites - which touches on applied category theory).  Poor data representation seems to have induced overbearing incidental complexity & compartmentalized algorithms, and I've been underwhelmed with the quantity of information that can be abstracted to the bootstrapped knowledge library.  So very much complexity; some of it needed, some not.  Areas like totality are probably fine and necessary imports, but we also need to support learning them by tolerating crashing or non-terminating behavior, and assembling guards for them, via application of propagators, laws, and experimentation.  This means falling back to raw search, as needed and known. Critical to make it bootstrapping, and we haven't sufficiently gotten there yet; it's been the AI agents that are doing the majority of search and changing the code, not the kernel.  To this end, a very critical oversight in the work so far is that the budged for search was very limited, where we realisically should tolerate hours of search to derive algebras, propagators, laws, transformers, inverters (like autocorrelation) and higher-order functors – provided the search algorithm is not fundamentally big-O too expensive (for example, hypotheticalyl we'd need an efficient graph isomorphism test (not for AU of course), or e-graphs; switching to multi-threaded operation is alas not within scope, it's orthogonal to the design).  Very simply, nothing "very interesting" was findable in the small budgets with rigid enumeration without clean reentrancy and reflection, so it all had to be imported by the AI agents, or patches added to the kernel.
