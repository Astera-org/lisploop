# Words and verb tables: the full record of measurements

*This is the complete record behind §8.7 of `plan_v2c.md`, which gives the main results only. Section numbers refer to the plan. The reports named in Appendix D of the plan hold the commands and logs for every figure.*

The mechanism of §6.4 and §6.5 is the outside review's, and the review supports it with experiments of its own. Each of those was rebuilt independently before it was used here, except the experiment on reuse across tasks, which was rebuilt only in outline. Several questions the review did not ask were measured as well **[M]**. Everything in this section is standard-library Python, except one experiment that was run on a copy of the Lisp prototype of §8.1. That experiment is reported under "In the store prototype" and in the last finding under "Memory". The search experiments use a library of operators written by hand for the purpose. None of them runs on the kernel's own library. Source and logs are in `plan_v2c_audit/verify-quotients/` and `plan_v2c_audit/round3/`.

**The setting.** The library has 61 operators: 31 that take a list to a list (`sort`, `reverse`, `filter_pos`, `running_sum`, `map_adj_sub`, and the like), 6 that combine two lists, 9 reductions, 11 on integers, and 4 that take an integer and a list. A task is 8 rows of a hidden composition of a few of them. A *candidate* is one operator applied to classes already found, and evaluated on all the rows. The baseline, called *plain search* below, is bottom-up enumeration of terms by size that keeps one term per class and uses no guidance. Unless stated otherwise the vocabulary is six words listed by hand: the signs present in a list, the parities present, whether it is sorted in either direction, its length capped at 5, its length modulo 2, and whether it is empty, with the matching words on integers. Each operator is run on probe values and grouped, once, into a verb table from input labels to the output labels seen. A candidate is skipped, before it is evaluated, when its tables say it cannot give the target's labels within the size that remains. This test is the *label filter*. The size of the answer is not known, so the search is repeated in *rounds*. The size bound is raised by one each round, each round starts again from the leaves, and the candidates of every round are counted. A *gain* is the number of candidates plain search evaluates divided by the number the method evaluates. Each experiment below has its own set of tasks, so the gain of the six hand-listed words is not one number. Among the figures quoted below for whole sets of tasks it runs from 13.9× to 18.5×, which the rest of this document gives as 14× to 18×.

**One routine for the counting cases.** The review claims that laws, inverses, near misses, exact facts about labels, and verb tables are all one group-by (§6.2). The claim was tested by writing that routine once and running it on each case. The routine is 30 lines. It groups the rows of a space by some columns, seen through a word if one is given, looks at another column within each group, and returns the table with its counts.

- *Verb tables and exact facts about labels need nothing more.* For six operators under three words, the 18 tables the routine returned were identical to the tables the search experiments use. Under residues modulo 5 it returned an exact table of 25 entries for each of `+`, `−`, and `*`, and it showed that `div` and `max` have no exact table.
- *An inverse needs two small searches on top.* Given the table of `*` on a grid, the routine reports that an argument is determined by the result and the other argument on 92.3% of the rows, as in §8.2. That is the first step only. The guard `(not (= b 0))` was then picked, as the weakest guard that is valid, from a pool of thirteen guards supplied by hand; that took 9 further lines. The term `(div c b)` was found by a search of 17 candidates; that took 18 further lines.
- *A law needs a different computation.* That nothing is left over is also true of `x + 1` given `x`, which are not equal. A law is two terms with the same column, and the store finds those by hashing columns into classes. Testing for equality took 2 further lines, and enumerating the terms took 15.
- *A near miss is not a counting case at all.* On eight rows, grouping by a candidate's values left nothing over for a near miss, and it equally left nothing over for a far-off term whose values are all different, in all of 2,000 random trials. In the banks of the 234 tasks used below, the target was "determined" in this sense by 54–56% of all classes on eight rows, and by 81% when one of the two task sets was cut to four rows. So counting says nothing about nearness. Measuring a near miss took 23 further lines of another kind: the element-by-element difference, the same difference taken per position for lists, the integer code, and a guard for the rows that disagree.

Plain search is averaged over random orders of the operator list, because the order matters: with the library in its listing order the first row is 13.9× and 16.7×. With the filter on, also ordering the operators of the last size by their tables changed the totals by only 3% to 5%.

One control is missing from every Python run in this section. It is the plainest one: compare each candidate of the last size with the target one row at a time, stop at the first mismatch, and use no tables. In the Python search a candidate is always evaluated on all eight rows. In the store prototype that control cut candidates exactly as much as ordering by labels did, and took less time (see "In the store prototype" below). Whether it would match the gains in this table is not measured.

Four qualifications apply.

- *Time gains are smaller than candidate gains.* Each candidate that survives costs about seven table look-ups. Wall time improved by 1.3×, 2.0×, and 4.2× at program sizes 4, 5, and 6.
- *In candidates the gain behaved as a constant factor.* On single-input chains found at sizes 3, 4, 5, and 6 it was 14.4×, 11.1×, 11.6×, and 15.6×, with no trend. The one case in which a gain rose with program size is the merged word described below, and there only against one of two baselines.
- *More and finer words give more.* One word gave 2.8× to 6.4×, three gave 6.1× to 12.6×, and all six gave 15.7×. Reading the labels on one row of the eight, with all six words, gave 5.9×.
- *Counts of past use fail when they set the order of enumeration.* Naive Bayes ranked the two held-out operators 51st to 58th of 61. The counts were then used as prices: each operator was given a cost that grows as its estimated probability falls, and terms were enumerated in order of total cost, so that a rarely used operator is reached late. That sent 5 of the 8 tasks that need one of the two operators past a cap of three million candidates. Used only to order the operators within each size, counts still gave 1.7× and 3.1× on those tasks.

**Answers are lost, and how many depends on the probes.** A table built from samples can be wrong in one way that matters: the probes saw a combination of input labels, and never saw one of the output labels that combination can produce. For example, `take3` of a list that never increases and whose first three elements are equal is a constant list, which is sorted both ways; that did not occur in 3,000 random probes. A candidate on the path to the answer is then skipped. With random probes, 34 of 300 tasks were lost at 100 probes per operator, 4 at 300, 1 at 1,000 and at 3,000, and none at 10,000.

The review proposes a rule for choosing probes, "*Stratify by current words*": spread the probes evenly over the labels that occur, and add edge cases such as boundary values and constant lists. Its own test of the rule used transforms chosen by hand to suit the words under test. Its figure of no misses in 200 tasks held for 7 of 20 probe seeds, and for none of 10 other task seeds. So four ways of choosing probes that use nothing written by hand about which cases matter were compared with each other and with the review's hand-chosen transforms.

A *run* in the table below is one search for one task with one set of tables. There were 900 runs on random tasks: 300 tasks, each searched with tables built from three different random seeds. There were 800 runs on tasks with one unusual row: two sets of 200 tasks, each searched with tables built from two seeds. In both of these sets the first of a task's eight rows is replaced by a structured list. In one set it is one of the review's own five lists, such as (3, 3, 3, 1) or (0, 0, 0). In the other it is drawn from ten wider templates, such as a constant list, zeros with one non-zero element, or an arithmetic progression; these were written after the probe rules had been fixed. The last column comes from a different measurement. It takes a stream of 4,000 random tasks and counts the tasks whose reference program contains a step that the tables wrongly call impossible, as a mean over ten table seeds. It is an audit of the reference programs, not a search. To *spread evenly over pairs of labels* means to group the probes by the labels their arguments have under each pair of words, and to give every group that occurs an equal share of the probes.

At 1,000 probes per operator, the rule in bold lost no answer in the 1,700 runs of the two middle columns together, which cover 700 tasks. Random probes lost 5 + 7 = 12 there, and the review's transforms lost 2 + 3 = 5. The rule in bold uses no knowledge of which cases matter, and at every number of probes it lost fewer answers than the hand-chosen transforms, or as few. The reason is that the inputs an operator meets inside a program are other operators' outputs. Sorted lists, constant runs, and two arguments that are related to each other then arrive without anyone listing them. The gain is the same under every rule, 17×. Building the tables took about sixteen times as long under the rule in bold as with random probes (the timings are under "Cost" below).

The tables are still not sound. On the streams of 4,000 tasks the rule in bold still met a wrong entry on about 5 tasks in 1,000 at 1,000 probes per operator, and on 0.55 in 1,000 at 3,000 probes, where only 4 of 10 seeds met none. So the fallback stays. Three related proposals were tested with it.

- *Feeding back the transitions of tasks solved by the fallback* is an idea the review first proposed and then dropped (its Appendix B.2). It removed nothing in the 1,700 runs once the probes were good, because nothing was lost there. On the streams of 4,000 tasks it did cut the rate at which wrong entries were met, after the first 1,000 tasks: from about 5.0 to about 1.7 per 1,000 tasks at 1,000 probes per operator, and from about 0.55 to about 0.13 at 3,000. That is three- to fourfold. It is left out of the first version, and it is a cheap thing to add later.
- *Merging sign, parity, and order into one word of 38 labels.* Used on its own, the merged word gave 22× where the six separate words give 17×. It still lost 10 of 600 answers at 10,000 probes per operator, which is ten times the number of probes at which the separate words lost none. It is also the one case in which a gain was seen to rise with program size, and that only against one baseline. On an earlier set of 60 tasks, with 2,000 random probes per operator, it lost 8 answers. On the tasks it solved, its gain against plain search averaged over operator orders was 12×, 23×, and 30× at program sizes 3, 4, and 5 (5, 22, and 24 tasks). Against plain search with the operators in listing order it was 22×, 24×, and 24×. It is left out.
- *Tables for two-argument operators must be built from probes that vary both arguments together.* The review proposed this rule. It supported the rule by building tables the other way, from slices, in which one argument is held at a few fixed values while the other varies. With the slices the review chose, those tables made 287 false rejections for `div` and 422 for `mod`, out of 1,476 checks: 492 fresh pairs with a non-zero divisor, each checked under three words. Counted by pair, 228 of the 492 true results for `div` and 293 for `mod` were rejected under at least one word. That evidence is weaker than it looks, for two reasons. The review's slices were a bad case, and most random choices of slice reject nothing. And at an equal budget of 100 probes, random probes of both arguments together rejected more than random slices did: a median of 14.6 per 500 pairs against 0. So the measurement does not decide the matter, and the rule is adopted on an argument. A table keyed on labels is a claim about every value that carries the label, and a slice samples only a few of them.

**What can be certified.** About 71% of table entries allow exactly one output label, as judged against the union of ten tables of 30,000 probes each and an exhaustive enumeration of short lists. A filter that uses only those entries has a gain of 10.2×, where a filter that uses every entry has 17.0×. Only the tables for length follow from anything that can be proved cheaply, and a filter that uses those alone has a gain of 3.6×. So everything beyond 3.6× rests on entries that are tested and nothing more. About two fifths of the factor rests on entries that allow more than one output label, where a label the probes missed cannot be told from one that cannot occur.

**A target the tables do not predict, and what a lost answer costs.** In §7.2 a search only reads the tables. That arrangement was checked for the one failure it invites: a task whose target carries a label that no table mentions. The check used 234 tasks of three and four operators, which are also the tasks of "Where labels act" below. No target carried a label that no probe value had shown, or that no operator's table could give as an output. That held at 100, 300, 1,000, and 2,000 random probes per operator. What did occur is the wrong entry described above: a step of the reference program whose input labels the probes saw and whose output label they did not. At 2,000 random probes per operator such a step occurred in 5 tasks, and the answer was lost in 3 of them.

Those three losses show why a per-task average flatters a filter. In that run the gain averaged per task was 16×. With the three lost tasks retried by plain search, the saving summed over all 234 tasks was 12.7×. Under the kernel's three-to-one reserve, where a task that is found costs four thirds of its filtered search and a lost task costs four times its plain search, the summed saving was 6.6×, against 12.1× under the same reserve had nothing been lost. Two of the three lost tasks were larger ones: they cost plain search about 312,000 and 319,000 candidates, where the geometric mean over tasks was about 44,000 and 41,000 in the two task sets. The third cost about 16,000.

**Where labels act, and where corrections do.** The review argues that labels look several steps ahead while corrections only see the last step. That was measured on the same 234 tasks, in two sets of 119 and 115. Call the class from which the answer is in fact built by one more operator the *true parent*, and call every class of the true parent's size a class *one step from the answer*, whether or not any operator takes it to the target. The figures about classes on the path to the answer use 179 of the tasks, those in which plain search found the reference program and not a shorter one. Where a figure compares the true parent with other classes one step from the answer, the other classes are all the classes of the true parent's size.

- *Labels act on the last step.* Among the classes one step from the answer, the tables rule out 43–47%. Among classes two or more steps away, they rule out none. They never rule out a class on the path to the answer: 0 of the 410 such classes, at any distance, in the 179 tasks. In the same runs the label-filtered search did lose 3 of the 234 answers, the three of the paragraph above, because a wrong table entry ruled out the last step itself. Applying the tables throughout enumeration still doubles the gain over applying them only at the final size, 15.6× and 16.9× against 7.8× and 8.0×. The cause was not isolated. The likely one is that during enumeration the tables also skip, before they are evaluated, the candidates one size below the answer that cannot reach the target's labels in one more step, and that size is the largest of those below the last.
- *Corrections carry a signal at every distance: strong next to the answer, weak further out, and nowhere strong enough to cut on.* Rank every class of one size by the cost of the target given that class. One step from the answer the class on the path is in the best tenth in two cases of three, and its median is about the 2nd percentile. It is in the worse half in about one case of five, so its mean is the 20th to 23rd percentile. Two and three steps out its mean is the 29th to 39th percentile and its median the 23rd to 41st, where the 50th is chance. That is too weak to cut on: keeping only the better half at each size lost 31 of 119 answers in one task set and 37 of 115 in the other.
- *The difference from which an offset or a scale is read is often not defined.* The correction code itself is defined for any two values, because it can pay for a change of length or fall back to the plain code of the target. What is often not defined is the element-by-element difference between a class and the target. That needs two values of the same type and the same length. It was undefined for 41–43% of the classes on the path one step from the answer, and for 74–90% of those two or three steps away. Where the types differ, the correction code falls back to the plain code of the target and so ranks nothing; that was so for about one true parent in ten.
- *Next to the answer the correction breaks some of the labels' ties.* With six words the labels leave the true parent tied with other classes for a quarter of the true parents, 26 of 102 and 26 of 99. Inside those ties the correction ranks it first in 12 and 14 of the 26 cases in the two task sets, where chance would give about 10 and 9. Only this direction was measured.

**Proposals from near misses.** A near miss is a class whose column is close to the target. Two proposals can be read off it (§7.2), and a third idea was tested for ranking near misses.

- *An offset or a scale.* 192 tasks were built so that the answer is a stored class plus or times a constant: two sets of 96, each with 64 offsets and 32 scales. The check compares a class with the target and, where the two have the same type and length, reads a constant offset or scale off their element-by-element difference. Only those two kinds of residual were tried. Each figure belongs to one arrangement of the check, and the arrangements differ.
  - *Inside the label-filtered search, on the classes the filter keeps.* This is the review's arrangement. It evaluated 2.4 to 3.0 times fewer candidates than the label-filtered search alone, which had the needed constant as an extra operator, or 1.6 to 2.0 times fewer when each check is itself counted as a candidate. No answer was lost.
  - *Inside the label-filtered search, on every candidate that is evaluated,* including those the labels then decline to keep. This halved the candidates again, by 2.07× and 1.97×, compared with checking only the classes the filter keeps. The reason is plain: a check that sees every candidate fires no later than one that waits for the filter, and when it fires the search ends. This is the arrangement of §7.2.
  - *In a plain search, with the check and no labels.* This evaluated 30 to 35 times fewer candidates than a plain search that has the needed constant as an extra operator, or 19 to 23 times fewer if each check is counted as a candidate. It took 1.6 times more candidates than the review's arrangement.

  On the ordinary tasks, which were not built to contain a near miss, some class smaller than the answer differed from the target by a constant offset or scale in 70 of the 234, and the program proposed from it was right on fresh rows in 64 of those 70. Once labels are in use the proposal adds little on these tasks: an ordering of the last step that always tried the right candidate first would add only 1.1× there.
- *A conditional.* On 120 tasks built as conditionals, the first split that fits is right in 82. On the ordinary tasks a split that fits the rows exists in 64, and the smallest is right in 2. A split that fits is nearly always an impostor unless something smaller has been ruled out first, which is the size-order rule of §8.5 again. Whether a label filter hides the classes that such a split is built from was also measured on these 120 tasks, and is reported with the prototype's result below.
- *Regularity of the residual's labels.* The review proposed to rank a near miss higher when the labels of its residual are the same on every row. In each of the two sets this puts the offset near miss first in 56 of the 64 offset tasks, where the size of the correction alone does so in 29 and 22. It does little for a scale: first in 4 and 3 of the 32 scale tasks, against none by the size of the correction alone. On the ordinary tasks it moved the true parent up in 47 cases and down in 62. On the tasks built as conditionals it moved the right branch down in 79 of 240 cases and up in none. It is a test for constant offsets and not a general ranking.

**In the store prototype.** The label filter was also built into the Lisp prototype of §8.1, with parity, residue modulo 3, and sign as the words, and run on its lifted sequence tasks and on the kernel's spec rows. Three findings.

- *A filter applied strictly at one size destroys guard splits, and rounds of increasing bound did not.* A near miss fails the labels on exactly the rows it gets wrong, so a filter that skips every candidate whose labels do not match skips the near miss too. In the prototype such a filter was applied at the one size where the near miss sits. That size was handed to it, and the search stopped there. The filter lost the conditional in all 9 runs in which plain search builds one. When candidates that return a Boolean were exempted, so that the guard itself survives, it still lost the conditional in 7 of the 9. One of the nine conditionals was the 29/200 impostor of §8.5, so eight right answers were at stake. In Python, with a size bound raised round by round, the loss did not occur. On the 120 tasks built as conditionals, both branches of all 120 conditionals were kept one or two rounds after plain search would first hold them. The cost was no higher: about 1,330 candidates by geometric mean with the filter or without it. That test asked whether both branches were kept. It did not ask whether the guard was. §7.2 uses the second arrangement. Whether the rounds let near misses through inside the kernel as they did in Python is not known (§10, risk 14).
- *Used only to order the candidates of one size, labels gain nothing that a plainer test does not.* The prototype builds its bank once, one size at a time, and keeps every class of a size for the sizes after it. It does not start again from the leaves at each new bound, as the Python search does. So a candidate skipped at one size would be missing if that size turned out not to be the last, and the filter can only reorder: the candidates whose labels fit are evaluated first, and the rest afterwards unless the answer has been found. With the prototype's own order of operators, reordering cut candidates 17× and 22× on `reverse` and `rotate`, 2.5× and 6× on `map-adjacent`, and nothing on the guarded tasks. Those figures are set by the order of the operator list: over six orders the cut on `reverse` ran from 1.04× to 23×. Comparing each candidate with the target one row at a time and stopping at the first mismatch, with no labels, gave the same cut in less time. Over the prototype's 15 tasks both cut candidates 2.73×, and the plain comparison saved 2.01× in time where the labels saved 1.68×. This is the control that the Python runs lack.
- *Labels pay as a look-ahead, once a firm size bound exists.* A *look-ahead* is the test of whether the target's labels and type can still be reached from a class within the size bound. A class that fails it is not stored, and an operator whose result type cannot lead to the target is not enumerated. When a program that fits is already held, its size bounds the rest of the search, and under the size-order rule a held split supplies that bound. On seven guarded runs this cut candidates 36×, time 17×, and the classes stored 34× (from 14.36 million to 0.42 million), and changed no answer. The seven are five functions, two of them run again on the kernel's spec rows. They are not the nine runs of the first finding, which also include the kernel's `map-adjacent` and `last-or` rows. The plain search of one of the seven stopped at its budget of 15 million candidates, so for that run the figures are lower bounds. Types alone gave the whole saving on five of the seven. Labels were needed on the two runs of the one function whose answer has a function call at its root, `scan` with a function argument. The bound has to be the size of the whole answer: with the size of a conditional's branches as the bound, the look-ahead discarded the branches in all nine runs of the first finding.

**Choosing the vocabulary.** Candidate words are small terms whose value is one of at most eight labels. Two candidate words that sort the probe values into the same groups count as one, so `(mod (+ (* 3 x) 1) 4)` and `(mod x 4)` are one candidate word. Each is scored by the dependency test. For one operator the score is the share of the uncertainty about the output's label that the input labels remove, and a word's score is the mean over the operators. The review calls this score lumpability. The four steps of the procedure are in §6.5. It was run in Python on this one library, with its thresholds set by hand, and with tables built from 2,000 random probes per operator. It has not been run together with the probe rule above.

- *On integers the score finds the expected words.* Over `+ − * neg` every `(mod x k)` scores exactly 1. Once `div`, `mod`, `max`, and `min` are counted, `(sgn x)` ranks first of 1,109 candidate words on a grid of −10 to 10 with floor division, and an arbitrary predicate such as `(= x 1)` ranks 702nd. The first place depends on those choices: with truncating division `(sgn x)` ranks 4th, and on a grid of −20 to 20 it ranks 2nd.
- *On sequences it reaches most of the hand list's gain by a different route.* The pool was every typed term of up to 5 nodes over the library's operators with `sgn`, `mod`, `<`, `=`, and small constants, together with two-part words made of two such terms of up to four nodes each: 95,732 candidate words. Words on lists were scored on the 37 operators that return a list. Choosing greedily by score, and skipping a candidate word that largely repeats one already chosen, gave 13.0× with six words and 15.3× with ten, against 15.8× for the six listed by hand. One answer of 60 was lost. A second probe seed gave 14.3× with six words. The six words chosen were all two-part words, and with single terms only the gain was 10.4×, with no answer lost. Without the step that skips repetition, in the earlier and smaller pool of `verify-quotients.md`, the fifteen best-scoring words were all versions of the length.
- *The thresholds are knobs.* The threshold for repetition was tried at three settings. They gave 14.8×, 13.0×, and 11.5×, with 1, 1, and 0 answers lost. Applying the repetition test to each of the two parts of a two-part word as well gave 12.3×, 13.9×, and 10.6× at the same three settings, with 1, 0, and 3 answers lost. The middle setting of that second version is the one §6.5 proposes to build for now, because it is the setting that lost no answer.
- *The words chosen are not the ones a person lists.* Nine of the ten have the length in them, usually joined in a two-part word with a property of an early element, such as "the length up to three, and whether the first element is negative". The hand list's words for signs and parities are in the pool and rank 4,401st and 3,866th of the 95,732; "sorted either way" cannot be written in the pool at all.
- *Several labels per word matter in search.* Allowing at most 2, 4, 6, or 8 labels per word gave 4.8×, 8.4×, 12.2×, and 13.0×.
- *Matching list words with integer words is the weakest step.* For five of the six list words, the integer word chosen was "is the value zero".
- *The grammar of candidate words does not need the library in it.* With the library's list operators in the grammar the gain was 13.0×. With only `len`, `head`, `last`, and the integer operators in it, the gain was 12.8×. With the library's operators and the fold forms the review named both in the grammar, it fell to 10.3×. As the library was grown from 26 to 45 to 61 operators, two of the six words carried over at each step, and the six words chosen at 26 operators were still worth 12.0× on the full library, against 13.0× for the six chosen at 61. With ten words the gain rose between the first two steps, from 13.5× to 15.3×, and stayed at 15.3× at the third.
- *Cost.* Building and scoring the pool took about 21 CPU-minutes, and choosing the words a further 5 to 20 seconds. For building the tables there are two timings, and they do not agree in absolute terms. In the earlier one, the tables for the six hand-listed words took 0.2 s at 2,000 random probes per operator, as the best of five runs. In the later one, with one run each on a loaded machine at 1,000 probes per operator, they took 1.1 s with random probes and 17.6 s under the probe rule in bold above. The ratio between the two rules, about sixteen, is the figure to keep.

**Memory.** Five findings. The first four are in Python, and the fifth is in the store prototype.

- *On a pure ring grammar, residues cut stored classes by about ten times.* On `+ − * neg`, reduction modulo a small prime commutes with every operator, so a class's residues can be computed from its children's before the class is evaluated, and a class whose residues cannot reach the target's within the size bound is not kept. With two variables, stored classes fell 8.5× to 26× on six polynomial targets of sizes 7 to 13, and 9.7× on average over 111 random targets of size 7 or more. None of 200 random targets was lost. Each class that is kept still stores its whole column; the saving is in keeping fewer. With `div` and `mod` in the grammar the residues no longer commute, and 117 of 120 targets that need them were lost. The largest of these searches holds 17,609 classes under plain search. Whether the saving holds near the memory wall of §8.4, at size 16 with three variables, was not measured; §7.6 gives the arithmetic of what it would be worth if it did.
- *The test must come before the class enters the table used for dedupe,* or that table keeps what the test rejected and the saving falls to 1.2× to 2.6×. When the size of the answer is unknown, the search starts again from the leaves at each new bound. The peak is then the last round, and the restarts cost, on average at each size, between 1.0 and 2.1 times the evaluations of that round, more at larger sizes. Keeping the bank across bounds instead needs a record for every class that was cut. Those records outnumbered the classes kept by 1.5 to 15 times, and the saving in bytes fell to 3.9× to 7.9×.
- *On the sequence library the saving from labels is small.* On the 61-operator library, stored classes fell 2.4× and bytes 1.5×. Bytes fall less than classes because each kept class also stores its labels. A *one-step look-ahead*, which also drops a class of the largest stored size when no one-argument operator can take it to the target, raises these to 7.1× in classes and 4.3× in bytes. Most of that further saving does not need labels. It comes from the classes of the largest stored size alone, which are 94% of what plain search stores on this library, and a rule with no labels reaches the same classes: hold them as hashes and drop their columns (§7.6). By calculation, and not by a run, that rule alone would save 5.8× in bytes, and labels would then add 1.3× to 2.5×.
- *With no target nothing is saved.* In a search for laws there is nothing to test against.
- *In the store prototype, the look-ahead cut the classes stored by ten times or more on lifted tasks, once a firm bound existed.* With the answer's size handed over, the classes stored fell from 25,639 to 270 on `reverse`, from 123,369 to 3,393 on `map-adjacent` with `+`, and from 2,306,369 to 34,919 on `map-adjacent` with a function argument. With no size given, the bound raised round by round, and a start from the leaves each round, the same classes were held at the end. The 34× of the seven guarded runs above is a saving of the same kind, with a held split supplying the bound. On the two of those runs that need labels, the classes stored were 49 and 17 times fewer with labels than with types alone. These figures cannot be compared with the Python ones. The two instruments count plain search's classes differently: the Python figures count the classes plain search stores below the answer's size, and the prototype counts what plain search holds when it finds the answer.

**Three smaller results.** The first is on guards. A guard is a two-label word and a Boolean column is a bit mask over rows, so "this guard is valid for this law" and "this guard implies that one" are single mask operations. For four laws, the weakest of 47 to 151 valid guards was found that way: `(<= b a)` for `max(a, b) = a`, and `(!= b 0)` for `div(a*b, b) = a`. A unique weakest guard exists only when some guard in the pool is true on exactly the rows where the law holds. A divisibility law had seven valid guards of which none is weaker than another.

The second is on reuse across tasks. In five streams of 20 tasks, with at most 19 stored solutions, replaying every stored solution on the new rows found all 25 repeats at no more than 57 candidates each. An index of past problems found 19 of the 25 with its best key, and 9 and 14 with two others. It found 20 when one-operator variants of the retrieved solutions were tried as well.

The third is on likeness between operators. Each operator was compared with the others of its type by the overlap of their verb tables. Of the 32 operators placed by hand in seven families, 22 have a family member as their nearest table, where chance gives 9. The result is uneven. The families of reductions and of zips score 5 of 5 each, where chance alone gives 2.5 and 4, and the family of running operators scores 0 of 3.

**What these measurements do not show.** Every Python search result above is on a library of whole-sequence operators written for the experiment, with six words that were also listed by hand except where the text says otherwise. None of the Python runs has the plain early-comparison control. The near-miss tasks were built to contain a near miss. The procedure that chooses words and the rule that chooses probes were each run on that one library, with thresholds set by hand, and never together. The prototype result is on element-level arithmetic, where parity alone does nearly all of what three words do. The four steps of §7.2 were each measured on their own and have not been run together as one search. Nothing here has been run inside the kernel.
